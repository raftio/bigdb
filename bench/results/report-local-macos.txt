The bulk load writes 19x fewer bytes. Both store the same records; the
difference is entirely that one commits in arrival order and the other does not.
The trade is memory: the whole load is held before any of it is written, which is why
a buffered ingest remains the right tool for a stream that does not end.

big: buffered ingest, capacity against shard count (50000 records, caller hands over 100 at a time)
bytes/record, with records per fragment per commit in brackets
  capacity          1 shard(s)          8 shard(s)         64 shard(s)        512 shard(s)
      1000          205 (1000)          1143 (125)           2979 (16)           26321 (2)
      5000           42 (5000)           223 (625)            503 (78)           4349 (10)
     25000           8 (25000)           37 (3125)            65 (391)            343 (49)
     50000           4 (50000)           22 (6250)            43 (781)            172 (98)

Read down a column and the cost falls with capacity; read across a row and it rises
with shard count. Both move the same underlying quantity - records per fragment per
commit, in brackets - so that is the first number to reach for when sizing a buffer:
decide how many records per fragment per commit you can afford, then multiply by
however many shards your ids actually spread across.

But it is not the whole model, and this table says so. capacity=1000 across 8
shard(s) and capacity=50000 across 512 shard(s) put 125 and 98 records in each
fragment per commit - within 28% of each other - and yet cost 1143 and 172
bytes per record, a factor of 6.7. The missing variable is not how many
fragment rewrites there were but what each one cost: a fragment holding tens of
thousands of records has dense containers, each owning a page that copy-on-write
rewrites whole, where a fragment holding a few hundred keeps them inline in a leaf.
So a buffer sized in records per fragment is the right first move and cannot be the
last one - see `where a commit's pages go` below.

`data` is b-tree pages - leaves, branches and the bitmap pages dense containers own.
The rest are chains a commit rewrites *whole*: the root records whenever any fragment's
root moved, the catalog whenever any zone map shifted - which every bit-sliced write
does - and the freelist on every commit without exception. Those three scale with how
many fragments the database has, not with how many this commit touched, which is why
they are the cost that batching cannot amortise and container-level cleverness cannot
reach.
